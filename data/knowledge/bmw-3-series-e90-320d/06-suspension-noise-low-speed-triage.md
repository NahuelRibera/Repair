# Clunks and knocks over bumps at low speed — cautious triage

Generic guidance for this demo project on describing a suspension noise
well enough to narrow down where to look. It does not identify a specific
worn part with certainty, since that reliably requires a physical
inspection (a technician manually loading and unloading each suspension
link while listening/watching for movement).

## Why the description matters more than the noise itself

Suspension noises are genuinely hard to diagnose from a description alone
— many different worn components can produce a similar "clunk." What
narrows it down is *when* and *where* the noise happens:

- **A single clunk over a sharp bump, from one corner of the car** — most
  consistent with a worn link, bushing, or end-link connecting the
  suspension to the anti-roll (sway) bar on that side, or a worn strut
  top mount. These are common wear items on any car as bushings age and
  are not by themselves a sign of anything unsafe, though they should be
  addressed reasonably promptly.
- **A clunk when turning the steering wheel at low speed, especially
  combined with a bump** — worth mentioning specifically, since it can
  point toward the steering rack mounts or a tie rod end rather than the
  suspension itself.
- **A creak or squeak rather than a clunk**, especially in cold or damp
  weather — often a bushing needing lubrication or simply aging rubber,
  generally lower urgency than a clunk.
- **A knock that changes with braking** (a knock only when braking over a
  bump, for example) — can point toward a strut mount or a subframe bushing
  taking up play under the change in load, rather than a static component
  failure.

## Questions worth asking before forming a hypothesis

- Which corner (or corners) of the car does it seem to come from?
- Does it happen over every bump, or only sharp/sudden ones?
- Has the car's ride height or handling changed at all (a sagging spring
  or failed strut can accompany a noise complaint)?
- Was any suspension work done recently — sometimes a noise appears simply
  because a bolt was not fully torqued during a previous repair.

## What is safe for an owner to check

- Visually inspecting (without jacking up the car) for obviously torn
  rubber boots or visibly disconnected/hanging hardware near a wheel.
- Pressing down firmly on each corner of the parked car and releasing, to
  get a rough sense of whether the ride feels noticeably different
  corner-to-corner (bouncing more than once or twice after release can
  suggest a worn shock/strut, though this is a rough indicator, not a
  measurement).

## What this does not cover

This document does not include the specific torque values, part numbers,
or inspection procedure a technician would use — that depends on the exact
suspension design and generation of the vehicle and should come from an
official service reference or a qualified technician's inspection.
