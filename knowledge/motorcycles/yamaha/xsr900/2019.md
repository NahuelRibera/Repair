## Quick specifications

- Cylinder layout: inline 3-cylinder.
- Displacement: 847 cm³.
- Bore × stroke: 78.0 × 59.1 mm.
- Compression ratio: 11.5:1.
- Electric starting system.
- Idle-speed reference: 1100–1300 rpm.
- Spark plug: NGK CPR9EA9.
- Spark-plug gap: 0.8–0.9 mm.

- Oil change quantity: 2.40 L.

## Break-in and early ownership

- Main break-in period: 0–1,600 km.
- 0–1,000 km: avoid prolonged operation above 5,600 rpm.
- 1,000–1,600 km: avoid prolonged operation above 6,800 rpm.
- Avoid prolonged full-throttle operation or sustained conditions that overheat a new engine.
- At 1,000 km, engine oil and oil-filter replacement are separate service items and should be recorded separately.
- After 1,600 km, normal operation is allowed, but sustained operation in the tachometer red zone remains undesirable.

## Pre-ride inspection

### Fluids and visible leaks

- Check engine-oil level before riding when practical, using the correct upright/level attitude.
- Look for fresh oil around the drain area, filter area, engine cases and beneath the motorcycle.
- Check coolant level when the engine is cold enough for a reliable reservoir reading.
- Look for dried coolant residue or wetness at hoses, radiator joints and the water-pump area.
- Check front and rear brake-fluid levels and investigate unexplained loss rather than repeatedly topping up.
- Check for fuel smell or visible leakage before starting.

### Controls

- Confirm smooth throttle movement and automatic return.
- Confirm clutch movement is smooth and free play remains within its specified range where applicable.
- Confirm front and rear brakes build firm, predictable pressure.
- Confirm brake and shift pedals move smoothly and return.
- Turn the handlebar from lock to lock and check for binding, cable tension or rough steering.
- Confirm sidestand movement and engine-cutoff/interlock behavior are normal.

### Chain and rear wheel

- Check chain lubrication, corrosion, damaged rollers/plates and obvious tight spots.
- Check chain measurement against the exact value below.
- Confirm both adjusters indicate consistent rear-wheel alignment after adjustment.
- Inspect sprocket teeth for hooking, asymmetrical wear or damage.
- A chain that has tight and loose sections around one wheel revolution may be worn even if average slack is in range.

### Tires and wheels

- Measure pressure before the tires heat from riding.
- Inspect tread and sidewalls for cuts, cracking, bulges or embedded objects.
- Inspect wheels for bends, impact damage or abnormal runout.
- After tire replacement, recheck pressure and ride conservatively until the new tire surface is scrubbed in.

### Lighting and instruments

- Check headlight, brake/tail light, turn signals and license-plate light.
- Confirm warning indicators complete their normal startup/self-check behavior.
- Treat a newly persistent warning as a diagnostic clue, not as a normal part of ownership.

- Engine oil: first service at 1,000 km, then every 10,000 km; annual condition also applies.
- Oil filter: 1,000 km, then every 20,000 km.
- Valve clearance: 40,000 km.
- Air-filter replacement: 40,000 km.
- Brake fluid: 2 years.
- Brake hoses: 4 years or sooner if damaged.
- Coolant replacement: 3 years.
- Drive-chain cleaning/lubrication: every 1,000 km and after washing, rain or wet riding.

- Initial service point: 1,000 km.
- Main recurring distance points: 10,000 km, 20,000 km, 30,000 km and 40,000 km.
- Annual checks remain relevant when the annual condition is reached before the next distance point.
- After 50,000 km, repeat the service pattern beginning with the 10,000 km column.
- Wet, dusty, dirty or unusually harsh use can justify earlier inspection of the chain, air filter, brakes, bearings and exposed chassis parts.

## Engine oil

### Level check

- Check on level ground with the motorcycle positioned upright as required for a representative reading.
- Bring the engine to normal operating temperature when the procedure requires a warm check, then stop the engine and let the oil settle.
- Keep the level between the minimum and maximum marks.
- Do not overfill. Excess oil can create aeration, leakage or breathing-system problems.
- If oil level repeatedly falls, investigate leakage or consumption instead of treating repeated top-ups as normal maintenance.

### Change quantities

- Oil only: 2.40 L.
- Oil with filter: 2.70 L.
- Oil baseline: SAE 10W-40; API SG or higher; JASO MA.

### After an oil change

- Confirm the drain area and filter area are dry before starting.
- Run the engine briefly and inspect for leakage.
- Stop the engine, allow the oil to settle and verify final level.
- Record mileage and date.
- Record oil and filter separately if only one of them was serviced.
- Do not infer that a filter was replaced merely because oil was changed.

### Early inspection triggers

- Oil-pressure warning that remains on after startup.
- Metallic noise that appears immediately after oil work.
- Fresh leak at the drain/filter area.
- Oil level above maximum or below minimum after a correctly performed check.
- Milky oil, strong fuel smell in the oil, or unexplained consumption.

## Oil filter and service fasteners

- Spark plug: 13 N·m.
- Oil-filter cartridge: 17 N·m.
- Engine-oil drain bolt: 43 N·m.
- Rear axle nut: 150 N·m.
- Chain-adjuster locknut: 16 N·m.
- Use a sound sealing washer/gasket at the drain point when applicable.
- Never compensate for damaged threads by increasing torque.
- If a torque value is not present in this file, treat it as unavailable rather than reusing one from another year.

## Spark plugs

- Type: NGK CPR9EA9.
- Gap: 0.8–0.9 mm.
- Tightening torque: 13 N·m.
- Inspect deposits, electrode erosion and insulator condition during scheduled checks.
- A wet plug after repeated failed starts points toward fuel delivery without successful combustion; a completely dry plug can point toward lack of fuel delivery.
- Uneven plug appearance between cylinders can be a clue to a cylinder-specific fueling, ignition or mechanical issue.
- Avoid using plug color alone as a definitive diagnosis after short trips or prolonged idling.

## Air filter and intake

- Normal replacement interval: 40,000 km.
- The filter element is an oil-treated paper type on these configurations; do not clean it with compressed air.
- Replace earlier after repeated dusty, very wet or dirty operation.
- Check the air-box drain/check hose at scheduled services and clear accumulated material as required.
- Make sure the element is seated correctly before operating the engine.
- An incorrectly seated or absent filter can accelerate engine wear.
- A heavily restricted filter can contribute to poor throttle response, higher fuel consumption and reduced performance.

## Valve clearance

- Inspection/adjustment interval: every 40,000 km.
- Perform clearance work with the engine cold.
- Symptoms that justify earlier investigation include persistent abnormal top-end noise, difficult cold starting, unexplained loss of compression or poor running that cannot be explained by fueling/ignition basics.
- Do not diagnose valve clearance from noise alone.
- This is an advanced service item; exact clearance values and internal adjustment procedures are intentionally not inferred when they are absent from this file.

## Cooling system

- Coolant replacement: every 3 years.
- Reservoir capacity to maximum mark: 0.25 L.
- Radiator/circuit capacity: 1.93 L.
- Check level with the engine cold.
- Inspect hoses, joints, radiator and reservoir for leakage or damage.
- Never remove a pressurized radiator cap from a hot system.
- If water is used temporarily in an emergency, restore the correct coolant mixture promptly.
- Repeated coolant loss, bubbling, overheating or fan behavior that has changed markedly deserves diagnosis rather than repeated topping-up.

## Fuel and intake system

- Tank capacity: 14.0 L.
- Reserve amount: 2.6 L.
- Fuel baseline: unleaded, E10 acceptable, RON 95 minimum/reference.
- Inspect visible fuel hoses for cracking, chafing and leakage.
- Keep drain/vent hoses routed without pinching.
- If the motorcycle runs poorly immediately after refueling, consider fuel quality/contamination before assuming a mechanical failure.
- A strong persistent fuel smell when parked warrants leak inspection before riding.

## Throttle and idle

- Throttle free play: 3.0–5.0 mm.
- Idle-speed reference: 1100–1300 rpm.
- The throttle must move smoothly and return automatically.
- Binding that changes with handlebar position can indicate cable/routing interference on cable-operated systems.
- Unstable idle can have many causes; verify battery voltage, intake integrity, fuel quality, air filter condition and stored warning information before assuming internal engine damage.

## Clutch

- Lever free play: 10.0–15.0 mm.
- Check operation and free play at recurring services.
- Lubricate the cable/control points where the configuration uses a serviceable cable.
- Too little free play can prevent full clutch engagement and contribute to slip.
- Excessive free play can prevent full disengagement and make shifting/neutral selection difficult.
- If free play is correct but the clutch still slips under load, investigate clutch wear or oil suitability rather than over-adjusting the cable.

## Brakes

### Fluid and hydraulic system

- Fluid: DOT 4.
- Replace brake fluid every 2 years.
- Replace brake hoses every 4 years or earlier if cracked, swollen, abraded or leaking.
- Keep fluid above the minimum level and investigate unexplained loss.
- Do not mix dirt or water into an opened hydraulic system.

### Feel and wear

- A soft/spongy lever or pedal can indicate air, fluid deterioration, leakage or another hydraulic problem.
- Check pad wear at both ends during scheduled service and whenever braking feel/noise changes.
- Replace pads before friction material is exhausted.
- After pad or wheel work, restore normal lever/pedal pressure before moving the motorcycle.
- Brake drag, pulsing, grinding noise or a sudden increase in lever travel deserves inspection before normal riding.

## Tires and wheels

- Front tire: 120/70 ZR17M/C (58W).
- Rear tire: 180/55 ZR17M/C (73W).
- Front cold pressure: 250 kPa / 2.50 bar / 36 psi.
- Rear cold pressure: 290 kPa / 2.90 bar / 42 psi.
- Check pressure cold and after any large ambient-temperature change.
- A slow pressure loss is a fault to investigate, not a reason to normalize frequent inflation.
- Inspect tread depth, cuts, punctures, sidewall damage, bulges and aging/cracking.
- Balance the wheel after tire replacement if required.
- Investigate steering vibration after a tire change by checking pressure, bead seating, balance, wheel damage and installation before altering suspension settings.

## Drive chain and sprockets

### Measurement

- Specified chain measurement: 5.0–15.0 mm.
- Check before riding and at least at the scheduled chain-service interval.
- Measure using the specified unloaded/sidestand condition for this configuration.
- Rotate the rear wheel and identify the tightest section before final adjustment.

### Cleaning and lubrication

- Clean, inspect, adjust and lubricate every 1,000 km.
- Lubricate after washing.
- Lubricate after riding in rain or wet conditions.
- Use a lubricant compatible with sealed/O-ring chains.
- Avoid harsh solvents that can damage chain seals.
- Wipe excess lubricant from surfaces where it could reach the tire or brake.

### Adjustment and alignment

- Rear axle nut: 150 N·m.
- Chain-adjuster locknuts: 16 N·m.
- Keep left/right adjuster positions matched for rear-wheel alignment.
- Recheck chain measurement after tightening the axle because final torque can alter the setting slightly.
- If correct slack cannot be maintained uniformly around the chain, inspect the chain and sprockets for wear rather than repeatedly adjusting one section.

### Replacement clues

- Hooked or asymmetrically worn sprocket teeth.
- Stiff or seized links.
- Repeated tight spots.
- Excessive adjustment required over a short distance.
- Rust, damaged plates, rollers or seals.
- Chain noise that persists after correct lubrication and adjustment.

## Wheel, steering and chassis bearings

- Check wheel bearings for play, roughness or noise during scheduled service and whenever a wheel is removed.
- Check steering bearings for looseness, notchiness or rough motion.
- A click felt under braking can come from steering-head play, brake hardware or fork movement and should be isolated rather than guessed.
- Inspect swingarm/linkage movement for play or binding.
- Later schedules include swingarm-pivot lubrication at 50,000 km; treat that as a major chassis service point where applicable.
- Recheck chassis fasteners at scheduled intervals and after any work that disturbed them.

## Suspension

### Front suspension

- Spring-preload reference: distance A 19.0 mm minimum/soft, 16.0 mm standard, 4.0 mm maximum/firm.
- Rebound reference on the adjustable fork leg: 12 clicks out minimum/soft, 7 clicks out standard, 1 click out maximum/firm.
- Adjust left/right spring preload equally.
- Count settings from the specified fully-seated reference without forcing an adjuster past its stop.

### Rear suspension

- Spring-preload range: position 1 minimum, 4 standard, 7 maximum.
- Rebound reference: 3 turns out minimum/soft, 1.5 turns out standard, 0 turns out maximum/firm.
- Restore the standard setting before diagnosing handling if the current setup is unknown.
- Preload changes ride height/sag; rebound changes the speed at which the shock extends after compression.

### Handling clues

- Excessive bouncing after a bump can point to insufficient damping or a worn damper.
- Harshness can come from excessive damping, too much preload, high tire pressure or mechanical binding.
- Excessive dive alone is not proof of a fork defect; consider braking force, preload, damping, oil condition and rider/load.
- Oil leakage from a fork or shock is a mechanical condition, not a setup preference.
- Always check tire pressure and mechanical condition before tuning suspension around a handling complaint.

## Lights and switches

## Cleaning and corrosion control

- Allow hot components to cool before washing.
- Avoid directing high-pressure water at bearings, electrical connectors, instrument seals, intake openings or chain seals.
- Use mild motorcycle-safe cleaners on painted/plastic surfaces.
- Remove road salt and corrosive deposits promptly.
- Dry the motorcycle thoroughly and lubricate the drive chain after washing.
- After washing, verify brake response at low speed because wet discs/pads may initially reduce braking performance.
- Do not contaminate tires, brake discs or pads with polish, oil or chain lubricant.

## Storage

### Short storage

- Park dry and clean where practical.
- Keep fuel system and battery condition in mind if the bike will sit for several weeks.
- Maintain tire pressure.
- Protect exposed metal from corrosion without contaminating brakes or tires.

### Longer storage

- Clean and dry the motorcycle first.
- Lubricate the drive chain.
- Keep the battery charged with an appropriate maintenance strategy.
- Reduce tire flat-spot risk by unloading or periodically rotating the wheels when practical.
- Protect cylinder surfaces and fuel system only with procedures appropriate to the expected storage duration and available equipment.
- Before returning to service, perform the full pre-ride inspection and verify fluid levels, tire pressure and brake operation.

## Basic troubleshooting

### Engine does not crank

- Confirm battery state and terminal tightness.
- Confirm stop/run switch and ignition state.
- Confirm neutral indication or the required clutch/sidestand interlock condition.
- Check the relevant ignition/start circuit fuse if the electrical system is otherwise alive.
- A single click with no crank can indicate insufficient battery current, poor connection or starter-circuit trouble.
- Do not keep holding the starter if cranking becomes very slow.

### Engine cranks but does not start

- Confirm adequate fresh fuel.
- Confirm the fuel pump/system primes normally where audible/indicated.
- Check whether plugs are wet or dry after repeated attempts only if safe and practical.
- Review recent work around fuel tank, plugs, air box or battery for disturbed connectors/hoses.
- A persistent warning indicator is relevant diagnostic context.
- Avoid repeated long cranking sessions that flatten the battery and complicate diagnosis.

### Difficult cold starting

- Check battery condition first; a marginal battery can crank yet still produce weak voltage during starting.
- Check plug age/condition and scheduled valve-clearance status.
- Check fuel quality and air-filter restriction.
- Do not assume valve clearance solely from difficult starting without other evidence.

### Engine runs hotter than usual

- Check coolant level only when cold.
- Check radiator face for blockage and obvious external damage.
- Confirm fan operation when temperature reaches the range that normally calls for it.
- Check for coolant leakage and oil level.
- Consider unusually slow traffic, high ambient temperature or sustained load as context.
- Stop riding if there is an overtemperature warning, steam, major coolant loss or abnormal mechanical noise.

### Cooling fan runs more often

- More fan operation can be normal in hot weather or slow traffic.
- Compare against coolant level, radiator airflow, warning indicators and actual temperature behavior.
- A fan that never runs during genuine overheating is more concerning than a fan that runs frequently.

### Brake lever or pedal feels soft

- Check fluid level and visible leakage.
- Check for recent brake work that could have introduced air.
- Inspect hoses for damage or swelling.
- Treat a sudden major loss of braking firmness as a safety-critical condition.

### Brake drag or wheel difficult to rotate

- Verify caliper/pad movement and wheel installation after recent service.
- Check lever/pedal free return.
- Heat buildup after a short ride can indicate dragging brakes.
- Do not continue normal riding with a severely dragging brake.

### Clutch slips

- Verify free play first.
- Check that the oil meets wet-clutch requirements.
- If free play and oil are correct, internal clutch wear may require deeper inspection.

### Shifting becomes difficult

- Check clutch free play and operation.
- Check chain adjustment and lubrication.
- Check shift-pedal/linkage movement.
- Verify engine oil level and condition.
- A sudden change immediately after maintenance should focus attention on what was disturbed.

### Chain becomes noisy

- Check lubrication.
- Check measurement/slack.
- Check wheel alignment.
- Look for tight spots and sprocket wear.
- Noise under only one load condition can be useful context but is not proof of one specific fault.

### Steering feels loose or notchy

- Verify tire pressure and tire condition first.
- Check wheel bearings.
- Check steering-head bearings for play/notchiness.
- Check front-wheel installation and axle hardware if the wheel was recently removed.
- Do not tune suspension around mechanical play.

### Motorcycle pulls after wheel or chain work

- Recheck rear adjuster alignment.
- Recheck tire pressure.
- Check wheel seating and brake drag.
- Confirm axle hardware was tightened correctly.

### Repeatedly blown fuse

- Verify correct fuse rating.
- Inspect the related circuit for pinched/abraded wiring, water ingress or a failed component.
- Do not fit a higher-value fuse to keep the circuit alive.

### Battery repeatedly goes flat

- Check terminal condition.
- Fully charge and test the battery.
- Consider charging-system output and parasitic draw if discharge repeats.
- Optional electrical accessories can materially increase discharge during storage.

## After-service verification

After any owner-level service, verify the work before normal riding.

- No tools or loose hardware remain on the motorcycle.
- All removed covers/seat parts are secure.
- No fresh oil, coolant, fuel or brake-fluid leakage is visible.
- Oil and coolant levels are correct.
- Brake pressure is firm.
- Chain measurement and rear-wheel alignment are correct after chain work.
- Tire pressures are correct after wheel/tire work.
- Handlebar turns freely without cable/wiring tension.
- Lights and relevant warning indicators behave normally.
- Begin with a low-speed function check before returning to normal traffic after brake, wheel, steering or control work.

## Expanded practical maintenance and diagnostics

- The sections below add qualitative owner-level maintenance, fault-isolation and service-planning guidance.
- Exact numeric specifications remain the model-year values stated earlier in this file.
- These notes do not create new model-specific torque values, capacities, intervals or dimensions.
- Condition, symptoms and confirmed maintenance history should be used together rather than relying on mileage alone.

### How to establish a trustworthy maintenance baseline

- When history is incomplete, separate what is known, what is unknown and what is only assumed.
- Record the current odometer independently from every historical service event.
- Start with safety-critical checks: tires, brakes, steering, wheel security, chain condition and fluid leaks.
- Then verify engine oil, coolant, battery condition, air intake and visible hoses/connectors.
- Use the exact intervals already listed in this file; do not infer a completed service from an interval alone.
- If a previous owner claims work was done but there is no confident mileage or date, keep it as uncertain history.
- After completing a baseline service, record each task separately so future due-state calculations are meaningful.
- Oil, oil filter, air filter, coolant, brake fluid and spark plugs should remain separate service events.
- For condition-based items such as tires or brake pads, record replacement mileage even when there is no fixed interval.
- When a service was completed earlier than scheduled, calculate the next due point from the actual completed event where appropriate.
- When an event mileage is higher than the current odometer, treat the data as inconsistent until clarified.
- When the user gives a relative statement such as “2,000 km ago,” resolve it only if the current odometer is known.
- A hypothetical mileage is planning context only and must never modify real vehicle state.
- A correction should replace or supersede the mistaken event only when correction intent is explicit.
- Older valid maintenance entries should remain in history even when a newer event exists.

### Maintenance priority when several items are due

- Prioritize faults that can affect braking, tire integrity, steering, wheel retention or loss of fluids.
- Next prioritize lubrication and cooling items that protect the engine from rapid damage.
- Then address chain/sprocket condition, battery/charging faults and overdue filters or plugs.
- Cosmetic work should not delay a safety-critical repair.
- An overdue interval does not automatically mean the component is failed, but it increases the need for inspection or service.
- A condition-based item can require immediate replacement even if mileage is low.
- A recently replaced part can still be faulty if installation, compatibility or surrounding components are wrong.
- After multiple services in one visit, verify each system independently before the first normal ride.
- If a symptom appeared immediately after maintenance, inspect the disturbed area before assuming an unrelated failure.
- If two plausible faults exist, prefer simple non-invasive checks before disassembly.
- Do not suppress warning symptoms by only resetting reminders or clearing stored information.
- Keep safety-critical uncertainty visible rather than presenting a guessed diagnosis as confirmed.

### Severe-use modifiers

- Frequent short trips can leave the battery undercharged and may increase moisture accumulation in the engine and exhaust.
- Repeated stop-and-go traffic increases cooling-system and fan workload.
- Frequent rain or washing increases the importance of chain lubrication and corrosion checks.
- Dusty riding increases air-filter loading and can justify earlier inspection or replacement.
- Very hot weather increases cooling-system stress and makes correct tire pressure and oil level especially important.
- Cold weather reduces battery cranking performance and changes cold tire pressure.
- Heavy luggage or a passenger increases tire, brake, suspension and chain load.
- Aggressive acceleration and engine braking accelerate chain, sprocket, clutch and rear-tire wear.
- Frequent high-speed riding raises tire temperature and places sustained load on lubrication and cooling systems.
- Repeated hard braking raises pad, disc and fluid temperatures and may shorten service life.
- Long storage makes battery state, fuel quality, corrosion and tire condition more important than mileage alone.
- After severe use, inspect condition rather than waiting only for the normal distance interval.

### Condition-based replacement signals

- Replace a tire for structural damage, exposed cords, deep cuts, bulges or unsafe aging even if tread remains.
- Investigate repeated tire-pressure loss; topping up air is not a repair for an active leak.
- Replace brake pads before friction material is exhausted and before backing material can contact the disc.
- Inspect a disc that shows deep scoring, cracks, abnormal discoloration, persistent pulsing or thickness concerns.
- Replace a damaged brake hose regardless of the normal time interval.
- Replace a chain/sprocket set when wear, tight spots, seized links or hooked teeth make adjustment unreliable.
- A chain that needs frequent adjustment may be near the end of its useful life even if one slack measurement is acceptable.
- Replace an air filter that is damaged, oil-soaked incorrectly, severely contaminated or no longer seals properly.
- Replace a battery that cannot hold charge after charging-system and connection checks exclude external causes.
- Replace cracked, swollen, hardened or leaking coolant hoses before they fail under pressure.
- Replace damaged wheel bearings or steering bearings when play, roughness or notchiness is confirmed.
- Replace control cables or hydraulic components when movement is no longer smooth or reliable.
- Replace damaged electrical connectors or terminals when heat, corrosion or looseness prevents reliable contact.

### Tire wear-pattern interpretation

- Center wear usually reflects sustained upright use and can be accelerated by excessive pressure or long motorway riding.
- Shoulder wear can reflect frequent cornering, aggressive riding or a tire compound/use mismatch.
- Cupping or scalloping can be associated with pressure, damping, balance, braking load or worn chassis components.
- One-sided wear can point to road camber, alignment, suspension or bearing issues depending on pattern and severity.
- A stepped front tread pattern can create steering vagueness before legal tread depth is reached.
- Rapid rear wear after a power or gearing change should be considered together with riding style and pressure.
- A new vibration after tire fitting should trigger balance, seating, pressure and wheel-installation checks.
- A tire that loses pressure only after riding can indicate a heat-sensitive valve, bead or puncture issue.
- Do not diagnose suspension solely from tire appearance; confirm pressure and wheel condition first.
- Record tire model and replacement mileage because different tires can have very different wear behavior.

### Brake-system diagnostic detail

- A lever or pedal that feels soft immediately after service can indicate trapped air or incomplete pressure restoration.
- A control that slowly sinks under steady pressure can indicate an internal hydraulic leak.
- A firm control with poor braking can involve contaminated pads/discs, glazing or unsuitable friction material.
- Pulsation felt mainly through the lever can be related to disc/runout or wheel installation; confirm before replacing parts.
- A dragging brake after wheel service should trigger caliper, pad, axle and wheel-spacing checks.
- A wheel that becomes progressively harder to rotate as it heats can indicate hydraulic pressure not releasing correctly.
- Uneven pad wear can indicate caliper alignment, piston movement or slider issues where sliders are used.
- Brake-fluid color alone does not prove fluid condition; time interval and contamination risk remain important.
- After any hydraulic opening, verify pressure and leak-free operation before normal riding.
- If braking behavior changes suddenly, treat it as safety-critical even if no warning light is present.
- ABS faults do not make a hydraulic leak safe; base braking condition must still be verified.

### Chain and sprocket diagnosis beyond basic slack

- Measure slack at multiple wheel positions when wear is suspected because a worn chain can have tight and loose sections.
- A chain that is correct at one point can be dangerously tight at another point.
- Side-to-side kinked links, missing seals or rust around pins suggest deterioration beyond simple lubrication.
- Hooked sprocket teeth indicate wear and can accelerate damage to a replacement chain.
- Abnormal drivetrain snatch can come from excessive slack, worn cush-drive components, fueling behavior or poor low-speed technique.
- Noise that changes with chain lubrication points toward chain/sprocket condition but is not proof by itself.
- After rear-wheel removal, confirm chain alignment and brake operation before judging new noise.
- Do not intentionally run the chain tighter than the model-year specification to reduce noise.
- After wet or dirty use, remove abrasive contamination before adding fresh lubricant.
- Record chain and sprocket replacement together when both are replaced as a set.
- A new chain on heavily worn sprockets can wear rapidly and may run noisily.
- Check that lubricant has not contaminated the rear tire or brake components.

### Cooling-system fault isolation

- If temperature rises mainly in traffic but falls at speed, inspect fan operation and low-speed airflow first.
- If temperature stays high at speed, consider coolant level, radiator blockage, circulation and engine-load causes.
- A fan that runs frequently in very hot weather may be normal; compare with coolant level and warning behavior.
- A fan that never runs during genuine overheating requires electrical and fan-circuit checks.
- Coolant loss without an obvious puddle can leave dried residue at a slow leak point.
- A soft or collapsed hose when cold can indicate hose deterioration or pressure-system issues.
- A radiator with externally blocked fins can lose cooling capacity without any internal cooling fault.
- After coolant service, a new overheating problem can indicate trapped air or incomplete filling.
- Do not open the pressurized system while hot.
- Repeated overheating should be resolved before continued high-load riding.
- When coolant and oil contamination are suspected, stop normal use until the cause is identified.

### Battery and charging diagnosis

- A battery can show acceptable resting voltage yet still collapse under starter load.
- Slow cranking should trigger checks of battery charge, terminals, grounds and high-current connections.
- Repeated discharge after long rides points toward charging or battery-capacity problems rather than only short-trip use.
- Repeated discharge after parking points toward battery self-discharge or parasitic draw.
- Corroded or loose terminals can imitate a weak battery.
- A newly installed accessory can create charging-load or parasitic-draw problems even if it works normally.
- Intermittent electrical resets over bumps can indicate connection or ground issues.
- A fuse that fails repeatedly should lead to circuit diagnosis rather than a larger fuse.
- After battery removal, confirm terminal polarity and cable routing before reconnecting.
- During storage, maintain charge with equipment suitable for the installed battery type.
- A battery near end of life may become noticeably weaker in cold weather before failing completely.

### Post-service symptom tracing

- Symptom immediately after oil service: check level, drain sealing, filter installation and disturbed wiring first.
- Symptom immediately after air-box service: check filter seating, intake hoses, vacuum lines and electrical connectors.
- Symptom immediately after wheel service: check spacers, axle seating, brake drag, sensor wiring and alignment.
- Symptom immediately after chain adjustment: check slack at the tightest point and rear-wheel alignment.
- Symptom immediately after brake service: check hydraulic pressure, pad position, leaks and wheel rotation.
- Symptom immediately after battery work: check polarity, terminal tightness, grounds and relevant fuses.
- Symptom immediately after bodywork removal: check pinched wiring and forgotten connectors.
- New vibration after fastener work: recheck the disturbed mounting points before changing suspension settings.
- New warning indication after service: inspect the systems and connectors that were touched before clearing anything.
- New fuel smell after tank access: stop and inspect fuel connections before starting again.
- When several tasks were performed together, isolate the fault by the systems disturbed rather than by replacing unrelated parts.

### Useful maintenance records to keep

- Current odometer with date.
- Engine-oil change mileage/date.
- Oil-filter replacement mileage/date.
- Air-filter replacement or cleaning mileage/date as separate actions.
- Spark-plug replacement mileage/date.
- Valve-clearance inspection mileage/date and whether adjustment was required.
- Coolant replacement date and mileage.
- Brake-fluid replacement date and mileage.
- Brake-pad and tire replacement mileage with product/position notes when useful.
- Chain and sprocket replacement mileage.
- Battery replacement date.
- Any suspension service or bearing replacement.
- Fault symptoms, diagnosis and repair outcome for recurring problems.
- User preferences such as personal tire-pressure or suspension setup stored separately from baseline specifications.

### No-start diagnostic sequence

1. Confirm the kill/run control and normal start interlocks.
2. Check battery strength under cranking rather than only at rest.
3. Check main and ignition/injection fuse circuits.
4. Listen for normal relay/fuel-system activity where applicable.
5. Confirm fuel quantity and recent fuel quality.
6. Check whether plugs become wet or remain dry after repeated failed attempts if plug inspection is practical.
7. Inspect connectors disturbed by recent service.
8. Check warning indicators and stored diagnostic information before replacing components.
9. Separate no-crank, cranks-no-start and starts-then-stalls because they point to different systems.

### Rough-running diagnostic sequence

1. Establish whether the problem is present cold, hot or all the time.
2. Check recent fuel/refueling history.
3. Check air-filter and intake condition.
4. Review spark-plug service history.
5. Inspect accessible intake hoses and connectors after recent service.
6. Check for warning indicators.
7. Compare idle behavior with behavior under load.
8. If multi-cylinder, consider whether one cylinder is behaving differently before treating it as a whole-engine problem.
9. Avoid changing several variables at once because it makes the real cause harder to identify.

### Unexpected vibration diagnostic sequence

1. Determine whether vibration follows engine rpm, road speed or braking.
2. If road-speed related, check tires, wheels, bearings, chain and wheel installation.
3. If engine-rpm related, inspect engine/exhaust mounts and recent service areas.
4. If braking related, inspect discs, wheel installation and brake components.
5. Check whether the issue started immediately after tire, chain or bodywork service.
6. Check loose accessories before assuming a chassis fault.
7. Escalate a sudden severe vibration rather than continuing high-speed riding.

### Fuel-consumption change diagnostic sequence

1. Confirm the change over more than one fill when practical.
2. Check tire pressures.
3. Check chain condition and brake drag.
4. Review air-filter condition.
5. Review fuel quality and riding conditions.
6. Check whether luggage, passenger load or sustained high speed changed.
7. Check for fuel leakage or strong odor.
8. Consider warning indicators or running problems before treating consumption alone as a fault.

### Steering or handling change diagnostic sequence

1. Check cold tire pressures.
2. Inspect tire wear and damage.
3. Check wheel bearings and steering-head condition.
4. Verify wheel/axle installation after recent service.
5. Check chain adjustment and rear-wheel alignment.
6. Return suspension to a known baseline if settings were changed.
7. Check load distribution and luggage mounting.
8. Only then make controlled suspension adjustments.

### Planning a service from odometer and history

- Start from the actual current odometer, not from the mileage of the latest maintenance event.
- For each service item, identify the latest confirmed event of that exact service type.
- Apply the exact distance/time interval listed earlier only where an interval is verified.
- If history is missing, show the interval but keep the due state explicitly uncertain.
- For time-based maintenance, calendar age can make a low-mileage motorcycle overdue.
- For condition-based maintenance, use inspection findings rather than inventing a distance interval.
- When both distance and time apply, the first limit reached governs planning.
- When a user enters older service history later, preserve it and still select the highest valid service mileage as the latest event.
- Do not use message order or database insertion order as a substitute for event mileage/date.
- When mileage and date disagree, keep both facts and flag the inconsistency if it changes the due calculation.

### Before approving a completed maintenance entry

- Confirm the user described completed work, not a plan, question or hypothetical.
- Confirm the service type.
- Confirm event mileage when provided.
- If event mileage exceeds current odometer, request clarification.
- If the statement is uncertain, do not convert it into confirmed history.
- If several services are confirmed in one sentence, store separate events.
- If the statement is an explicit correction, correct only the intended prior event.
- Do not re-save unrelated facts that merely appear in conversation context.
- Do not emit a current-odometer update unless the user actually updated current mileage.

### After-service ride check

- Perform the first check at low speed and low load.
- Confirm braking feel before normal traffic speed.
- Confirm steering and wheel behavior after tire or wheel work.
- Listen for new chain, brake, bodywork or exhaust noises.
- Check warning indicators.
- Stop and inspect if a new fluid smell or leak appears.
- Recheck disturbed areas after the first short ride when appropriate.
- If behavior changed, investigate the serviced area before continuing normal use.

### Fluid-leak localization

- Identify the fluid before deciding which system is involved.
- Engine oil usually leaves an oily film and can collect dirt around the leak path.
- Coolant often leaves colored or chalky residue after drying.
- Brake fluid near a caliper, hose or master cylinder is safety-critical and should not be ignored.
- Fuel leakage or persistent raw-fuel odor should be resolved before normal operation.
- Clean the suspected area before rechecking so fresh leakage can be distinguished from old residue.
- Trace leakage upward because airflow and gravity can move fluid away from the source.
- A leak that appears only hot may depend on pressure or thermal expansion.
- A leak that appears after service should be checked first at the disturbed sealing points.
- Record repeated small leaks because trend information can reveal deterioration before a major failure.

### Bearing and pivot wear clues

- Wheel-bearing play can present as looseness, noise or rough rotation.
- Steering-head wear can present as notchiness around straight ahead or clunking under braking.
- Swingarm or linkage wear can create rear-end play or imprecise suspension response.
- A dry or seized pivot can make a control or suspension joint feel unusually stiff.
- Do not confuse tire carcass movement with confirmed bearing play; unload and inspect correctly.
- After wheel removal, verify bearing seals and spacers are undamaged and correctly positioned.
- Water and dirt exposure can shorten bearing life even when mileage is moderate.
- A bearing problem that worsens rapidly should be addressed before high-speed riding.
- Noise that changes with lean angle can help distinguish wheel/bearing issues from engine-speed noise.

### Fastener, vibration and mounting checks

- Investigate a new rattle by checking recently disturbed fasteners and adjacent brackets first.
- Bodywork noise can imitate mechanical noise at certain engine speeds.
- Exhaust shields and mounting points can create metallic rattles when loose.
- Accessory brackets can loosen even when the original motorcycle fasteners remain secure.
- Do not overtighten small fasteners to stop vibration; use the correct model-year tightening data where available.
- Repeated loosening can indicate missing locking hardware, damaged threads or excessive vibration.
- After a tip-over, inspect bars, levers, pegs, exhaust, controls and mounting points before riding normally.
- If a safety-critical fastener has uncertain torque and no verified value is present, treat the exact torque as unavailable rather than guessing.

### Water, corrosion and connector care

- After heavy rain, inspect chain condition and exposed electrical areas if new symptoms appear.
- Do not assume a connector is dry internally because the exterior looks dry.
- White or green corrosion on terminals can increase resistance and create intermittent faults.
- Road salt should be removed promptly because it accelerates corrosion on fasteners, brakes and exposed metal.
- High-pressure washing can force water past seals and into connectors.
- After washing, verify switches, lights and brakes before normal riding.
- Drying and lubrication are more useful than repeatedly spraying products onto dirty components.
- Corrosion around grounds can cause multiple seemingly unrelated electrical symptoms.
- A fault that appears only after rain should prompt moisture and connector inspection before expensive component replacement.

### Fuel quality and storage effects

- Fuel quality can degrade during long storage and cause hard starting or poor running.
- A symptom that begins immediately after refueling can be related to contaminated or incorrect fuel.
- Repeated short runs during storage can add moisture and discharge the battery without fully warming the motorcycle.
- Before long storage, address existing leaks or weak battery behavior rather than leaving them unresolved.
- On return to service, inspect fuel smell, hose condition and starting behavior before extended riding.
- If the motorcycle starts but runs poorly after storage, fuel, battery voltage and intake obstruction are sensible early checks.
- Do not repeatedly crank a motorcycle with suspected stale fuel and a weakening battery without narrowing the cause.
